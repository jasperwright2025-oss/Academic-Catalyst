"Your objectives are too broad." If you have heard that from a supervisor, you are in good company. It is one of the most common comments on a first proposal draft, and it is fixable in an afternoon once you know what examiners are looking for.

This guide walks you through writing a general objective, three to five specific objectives and the research questions that go with them. We use the SMART test throughout, and we work through two example topics from rough idea to finished objectives.

## What are research objectives, and why do they matter so much?

Your objectives are the promises your study makes. Everything else in the proposal hangs off them:

- your **research questions** restate each objective as a question you will answer;
- your **literature review** is organised around the themes the objectives raise;
- your **methodology** explains how you will collect and analyse data for each objective;
- your **findings chapter**, later on, reports back objective by objective.

That is why vague objectives cause trouble everywhere. If an objective says "to understand the role of mobile money", nobody (including you) can say what data you need, how you will analyse it, or when you are finished.

Most proposal formats ask for two levels:

1. **A general objective** (sometimes called the main or broad objective): one sentence that states the overall purpose of the study.
2. **Specific objectives**: usually three to five smaller, concrete steps that, taken together, achieve the general objective.

## What does SMART stand for?

The SMART idea comes from management, not academia. George T. Doran set it out in a short 1981 article in *Management Review*, where the letters stood for specific, measurable, assignable, realistic and time-related ([background on the SMART criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMART_criteria); [a short history](https://www.projectsmart.co.uk/brief-history-of-smart-goals.php)). The letters have been reworded many times since. For a research proposal, this version works well:

| Letter | Means | The question to ask of each objective |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S | Specific | Does it name exactly what, who and where? |
| M | Measurable | Could you say what data would show it has been met? |
| A | Achievable | Can you do it with your time, money, skills and access? |
| R | Relevant | Does it serve the general objective and the problem you stated? |
| T | Time-bound | Is the period of study or data collection clear? |

## Step 1: Start from the problem, not the title

Before writing any objective, write two or three sentences on the problem your study responds to. What is going wrong, or what is unknown, and for whom?

Here is a rough idea from an invented example student, a final-year business student:

> "I want to research mobile money and small businesses."

That is a subject area, not a problem. A short problem statement might read:

> "Many market vendors in Mbale accept mobile money, but it is not clear whether it has changed how they keep records or manage cash. Without this, local savings groups and lenders design their support on guesswork."

Now there is something to aim at: vendors, one town, record-keeping and cash management.

## Step 2: Write one general objective

The general objective usually begins "To assess", "To examine", "To investigate" or "To determine", followed by the main variables, the population and the setting.

**Weak:** To study mobile money and small businesses in Uganda.

**Better:** To examine the effect of mobile money use on record-keeping and cash management among market vendors in Mbale City.

The better version names the variables (mobile money use; record-keeping; cash management), the population (market vendors) and the place (Mbale City). It does not need a time frame; that usually sits in the specific objectives or the scope section.

## Step 3: Break it into three to five specific objectives

Each specific objective should cover one piece of the general objective. A useful pattern is: one objective to describe the situation, one or two to examine relationships or effects, and one on challenges or improvements if your design supports it.

For the mobile money example:

1. To describe the extent of mobile money use among market vendors in Mbale City.
2. To assess how mobile money use relates to the record-keeping practices of market vendors in Mbale City.
3. To examine how mobile money use relates to the cash management practices of market vendors in Mbale City.
4. To identify the challenges market vendors in Mbale City face in using mobile money for business.

### Verbs that help, and verbs that cause trouble

Action verbs make objectives measurable:

- **Helpful:** describe, identify, assess, compare, determine, examine, establish, estimate, analyse.
- **Risky:** understand, know, appreciate, explore the role of, look into.

"To understand" is the classic trap. You cannot measure understanding, and an examiner cannot tell when you have achieved it. "Explore" can be fine in qualitative work, but pair it with something concrete: "to explore how vendors decide which payments to accept by mobile money".

## Step 4: Run the SMART test on every objective

Take objective 2 and test it:

- **Specific:** names the variable (record-keeping practices), the population (market vendors) and the place (Mbale City). Pass.
- **Measurable:** what data would show it is met? Survey questions on whether vendors keep written or digital records, how often, and whether they use mobile money statements. Pass, as long as the methodology spells this out.
- **Achievable:** can one student reach enough vendors? In one town's main markets, probably yes. If the objective said "across Eastern Uganda", it would likely fail here.
- **Relevant:** it serves the general objective directly. Pass.
- **Time-bound:** not yet. Add the period: "...during the 2026/27 data collection period" or state it once in the scope section if your format prefers that.

If an objective fails a test, fix that part only. You do not need to start again.

## Step 5: Turn each objective into a research question

Your research questions should mirror your objectives one to one. Change the verb into a question, keep everything else:

| Specific objective | Research question |
| --- | --- |
| To describe the extent of mobile money use among market vendors in Mbale City | To what extent do market vendors in Mbale City use mobile money? |
| To assess how mobile money use relates to record-keeping practices | How does mobile money use relate to the record-keeping practices of market vendors in Mbale City? |
| To examine how mobile money use relates to cash management practices | How does mobile money use relate to the cash management practices of market vendors in Mbale City? |
| To identify the challenges vendors face in using mobile money | What challenges do market vendors in Mbale City face in using mobile money for business? |

If you are doing quantitative work, your department may also want hypotheses for the relationship objectives (here, 2 and 3), usually stated as a null and an alternative hypothesis.

## Step 6: Link each objective to a method

This is the step most students skip, and it is the one examiners like most. Draw a small table showing, for each objective, what data you will collect and how you will analyse it. For our example, objective 1 might use a questionnaire and descriptive statistics; objectives 2 and 3 a questionnaire and correlation or regression; objective 4 open-ended questions or a few interviews, analysed by theme.

If you cannot fill in the method column for an objective, that objective is probably too vague, or not achievable with your design.

## A second worked example: a qualitative study

Not every study measures relationships. Here is an invented education example.

**Rough idea:** "Something about why first-year students struggle with academic writing."

**Problem:** First-year students at one private university are often referred to the writing centre, but staff do not know which parts of academic writing students find hardest or why.

**General objective:** To explore first-year students' experiences of academic writing at [University X].

**Specific objectives:**

1. To identify the academic writing tasks first-year students find most difficult.
2. To explore the reasons students give for these difficulties.
3. To describe the support strategies students currently use.

**SMART check, objective 2:** specific (first-year students, one university, reasons for difficulty); measurable in a qualitative sense (you will know it is met when you have interview data on reasons, analysed by theme); achievable (a set number of interviews in one semester); relevant; and time-bound once you state the semester. In qualitative work "measurable" means "you can show what evidence answers it", not "it has a number".

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Objectives that are really methods.** "To administer a questionnaire to 120 vendors" is a step in your method, not an objective.
- **Objectives that promise a solution.** "To solve poor record-keeping" is beyond any single study. "To recommend ways to improve record-keeping" can work as a final objective if your findings will support it.
- **Mismatch with the title.** If your title says "effect" but your objectives only describe, your supervisor will spot it.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many specific objectives should a research proposal have?

Most undergraduate and master's proposals have three to five. Check your department's guidelines, but fewer, sharper objectives are usually better than many overlapping ones.

### What is the difference between a research objective and a research question?

They say the same thing in different forms. An objective states what you will do ("to assess..."); the matching research question asks what you want to find out ("how does...?"). Each objective should have one matching question.

### Can qualitative objectives be SMART?

Yes. In qualitative research, "measurable" means you can say what evidence would answer the objective, such as interview data on a stated theme. Specific, achievable, relevant and time-bound apply in the same way.

### Should my objectives include the time period?

Your study should be time-bound somewhere. Some formats put the period in each objective; many put it once in the scope or methodology section. Follow your institution's format and be consistent.
