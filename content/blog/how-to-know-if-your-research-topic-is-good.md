Most proposal trouble starts with the topic. If the topic is too broad, the objectives come out vague, the literature review has no edges and the methods cannot answer the question. Your supervisor then sends it back, and you lose weeks.

You are not alone in this. A study of master's students at Makerere University Business School found that students reported difficulty with identifying the research problem, reviewing the literature and choosing a methodology. The cause they agreed with most was a lack of proper guidance in research ([Eyangu, Bagire and Kibrai, 2014, *Creative Education*](https://file.scirp.org/Html/1-6302218_51899.htm)).

You cannot always get more supervision time. What you can do is arrive at each meeting with a topic that has already passed the basic tests. This guide gives you four checks, shows each one on a worked example, and ends with a checklist you can use tonight.

## The short answer

A good research topic is **clear** (one reader would describe it the same way you do), **original** (it adds something, even if small), **relevant** (someone in your field or community needs the answer) and **feasible** (you can finish it with the time, money, data and skills you have). If your topic fails any one of these, fix it before you write objectives.

## Check 1: Is it clear?

A clear topic names four things: **what** you are studying, **who** or what it involves, **where**, and **when** or under what conditions. If any of these is missing, a reader has to guess, and different readers guess differently.

**Worked example (invented):**

- Weak: *"The impact of mobile money on businesses in Uganda."*
- Better: *"The effect of mobile money use on the monthly sales of women-owned retail shops in Mbale City, 2024 to 2026."*

The weak version leaves every key word open. Which businesses? Which effect: sales, savings, record keeping? All of Uganda? The better version answers each question, so the objectives almost write themselves.

**Quick test:** read your title to a classmate and ask them to say, in their own words, what you will measure and where. If their answer differs from yours, the title is not yet clear.

## Check 2: Is it original?

Originality at proposal level rarely means "no one has ever studied this". It usually means you add one new piece: a different place, a different group, a newer period, a different method, or a variable earlier studies left out.

**Worked example (invented):** studies on the effect of school feeding on attendance exist in many countries. A topic such as *"School feeding and pupil attendance in government primary schools in Bulambuli District"* is original if you can show that published work has not looked at this district, this type of school, or this period.

**How to check:**

1. Search Google Scholar and your university repository with your main key words.
2. Note the five closest studies: where they were done, with whom, when and how.
3. Write one sentence: "Unlike these studies, mine looks at…" If you cannot finish that sentence, change one element of your topic.

Be honest about what your search found. No search can prove that a topic has never been studied, so say "I found no study that…" rather than "no study has ever…".

## Check 3: Is it relevant?

A relevant topic answers a question someone needs answered: a ministry, a district office, a hospital, a school, a business or other researchers. Supervisors often ask "So what?" This check is your reply.

**Worked example (invented):** *"Factors affecting the uptake of the HPV vaccine among girls aged 10 to 14 in Tororo District"* is relevant if you can point to a real gap, such as uptake that is lower than the district target, and to the people who would act on your findings.

**How to check:** write two sentences.

- "This matters because…" (the problem, with a source if you use a fact or figure)
- "The results could be used by… to…" (a named group and a named action)

If the second sentence is hard to write, your topic may be interesting but not yet useful.

## Check 4: Is it feasible?

This is where many good ideas fail. A feasible topic fits:

- **your time:** most master's students have one to two semesters for fieldwork and writing;
- **your money:** transport, data collection, research assistants and any ethics fees;
- **your access:** you can reach the people or records you need, and get permission;
- **your skills:** you can do, or can learn in time, the analysis the topic needs.

**Worked example (invented):**

- Too big: *"Climate change and food security in East Africa."* That is a career, not a dissertation.
- Feasible: *"Household coping strategies during the 2025 dry season among smallholder maize farmers in two sub-counties of Sironko District."*

The second topic can be done with one survey, a manageable sample and a clear period. It still connects to the big issue, but you can finish it.

**Quick test:** list the data you need, where it comes from, who must give permission and how long each step takes. If the total runs past your deadline, narrow the place, the group or the period.

## Putting it together: one topic, four checks

Here is one invented topic taken through all four checks.

**Starting idea:** *"Unemployment among graduates in Uganda."*

| Check | Problem | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clear | No group, place, period or outcome | Graduates of business courses, Mbale City, time to first job |
| Original | Many national reports already exist | Focus on one city and on the role of internships |
| Relevant | Who would use it? | Universities' career offices and internship partners |
| Feasible | National survey is too costly | A survey of about 150 recent graduates from two institutions |

**Final topic:** *"The effect of internship placement on time to first employment among business graduates in Mbale City."*

Notice that the final topic is narrower, but it is also stronger. Each objective now has one clear job.

## What to take to your supervisor

When supervision time is short, make each meeting count. Bring one page with:

1. Your topic in one sentence.
2. The four checks, one line each.
3. The five closest studies and how yours differs.
4. One question you need your supervisor to decide, such as which of two places to study.

This shows you have done the groundwork and lets your supervisor spend the meeting on judgement rather than basics.

## Frequently asked questions

**How narrow should my research topic be?**
Narrow enough that you can collect and analyse the data within your programme's time limit. If you can name the group, the place, the period and the one main outcome, you are usually close.

**My supervisor says my topic is too broad. What do I cut first?**
Start with the place, then the group, then the period. Moving from "Uganda" to one district, or from "students" to "first-year nursing students", often fixes most of the problem.

**Can I study a topic that has been studied before?**
Yes. Most proposals build on earlier work. Show clearly what you add: a new setting, a newer period, a different group or a different method.

**Is it acceptable to use a tool to check my topic?**
A checking tool can help you think, but the topic and the decisions must be yours. Follow your university's rules on AI assistance, and declare it where they require you to.
